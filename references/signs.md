# Signs of AI writing: field guide and fixes

This catalogue follows the structure and terminology of the supplied *De-AI writing.pdf*, which identifies its source as Wikipedia, *Signs of AI writing* (WikiProject AI Cleanup), September 2026 edition. Wikipedia-specific signs that only apply to wikitext, citation templates, categories, or edit summaries are outside this guide.

The signs are symptoms, not proof of authorship. Use judgement and context. Do not treat an isolated word or stylistic choice as evidence that a passage was written by AI.

## 1. Content

### 1.1 Undue emphasis on significance, legacy, and broader trends

Ordinary facts get a sentence about why they matter, how they “reflect broader” trends, or their “enduring legacy”. Even mundane subjects can be inflated.

**Words and phrases to watch:** “stands as”, “serves as”, “is a testament/reminder”, “a crucial/pivotal/vital/significant/key role or moment”, “underscores/highlights its importance”, “reflects broader”, “symbolising its ongoing/enduring/lasting”, “contributing to the”, “setting the stage for”, “marking/shaping the”, “represents/marks a shift”, “key turning point”, “evolving landscape”, “focal point”, “indelible mark”, “deeply rooted”, “has generated debate about”, “prompted broader reflection on”.

**Fix:** Delete the significance sentence, or replace it with a specific fact that shows the point.

Before: “The institute was established in 1989, marking a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional statistics.”

After: “The institute was set up in 1989. It was Spain's first regional statistics office.”

### 1.2 Canned emphasis on notability and media coverage

Importance is supposedly proved by listing where something was covered or describing the sources themselves.

**Words and phrases to watch:** “independent coverage”, “local/national media outlets”, “trade publications”, “cited/featured/profiled in”, “written by a leading expert”, “maintains an active social media presence”.

**Fix:** Say what the thing is or did. Cite coverage only as a source for a specific claim.

### 1.3 Superficial analysis

A participle clause is attached to a factual sentence and interprets it without adding information, such as “…, highlighting its importance”, “…, ensuring…”, or “…, reflecting…”.

**Words and phrases to watch:** “highlighting”, “underscoring”, “emphasising”, “ensuring”, “reflecting”, “symbolising”, “contributing to”, “cultivating”, “fostering”, “encompassing”, “enhancing”, “showcasing”, “valuable insights”, “align/resonate with”.

**Fix:** Cut the clause. If the interpretation matters, make it a separate sentence supported by evidence.

Before: “The station has 8 tracks and 6 platforms, reflecting its continued relevance in the regional transportation landscape.”

After: “The station has 8 tracks and 6 platforms.”

### 1.4 Promotional, advert, or travel-brochure language

Neutral subjects are written up like a hotel listing or press release.

**Words and phrases to watch:** “boasts a”, “vibrant”, “rich” (culture/heritage/history), “profound”, “enhancing”, “showcasing”, “exemplifies”, “commitment to”, “natural beauty”, “nestled”, “in the heart of”, “groundbreaking”, “renowned”, “featuring”, “diverse array”, “breathtaking”, “stunning”, “world-class”, “seamless”, “state-of-the-art”.

**Fix:** Replace the adjective with the fact that would earn it, or drop it.

Before: “Nestled in the heart of the old town, the café boasts a diverse array of pastries.”

After: “The café is on the main square and sells about twenty kinds of pastry.”

### 1.5 Vague attributions and overgeneralised opinions

Opinions are attributed to unnamed authorities, or one source is presented as if it were many.

**Words and phrases to watch:** “industry reports”, “observers have cited”, “experts argue”, “some critics argue”, “several sources” when there are one or two, “studies show”, “widely regarded”, and “such as” before what is actually a complete list.

**Fix:** Name the source, state the claim as your own view where appropriate, or cut it.

### 1.6 Formulaic “challenges and future prospects” endings

A piece says “Despite its [praise], X faces several challenges…” then “Despite these challenges, X continues to thrive…”, often in a closing “Challenges” or “Future outlook” section.

**Fix:** State a real problem plainly where it belongs. Drop the optimistic wrap-up.

### 1.7 Leads that define the title as a thing

A lead begins with “X refers to…” or “The List of Y is a curated compilation of…”.

**Fix:** Start with the subject itself. For example: “A catchment area is the area a hospital draws patients from.”

### 1.8 “X and Y” headings, such as “Awards and recognition”

Paired headings are sometimes added to sound complete. Keep a heading only if the section needs it.

## 2. Language and grammar

### 2.1 High density of “AI vocabulary”

One or two of these terms is normal. Several in one piece can be a stronger stylistic warning.

**2023 to mid-2024 set:** “additionally” (especially at the start of a sentence), “boasts”, “bolstered”, “crucial”, “delve”, “emphasising”, “enduring”, “garner”, “intricate/intricacies”, “interplay”, “key” as an adjective, “landscape” in an abstract sense, “meticulous”, “pivotal”, “underscore”, “tapestry”, “testament”, “valuable”, “vibrant”.

**Mid-2024 to 2025 set:** “align with”, “bolstered”, “crucial”, “emphasising”, “enhance”, “enduring”, “fostering”, “highlighting”, “pivotal”, “showcasing”, “underscore”, “vibrant”.

**Newer models:** “emphasising”, “enhance”, “highlighting”, “showcasing”.

**Fix:** Use the ordinary word or cut the phrase. “Additionally” may be unnecessary; “crucial” may be “important”, or the reason may need explaining; abstract “landscape” can be replaced with the actual subject. Do not just swap in a synonym. The padding or vague claim may be the real problem.

### 2.2 Avoiding “is”, “are”, and “has”

A plain verb is dressed up unnecessarily.

**Words and phrases to watch:** “serves as”, “stands as”, “marks”, “functions as”, “operates as”, “represents”, “boasts/features/maintains/offers” when used to mean “has”, “refers to”, “ventured into politics as a candidate” when the person was a candidate, and “began his career as” when “was” is accurate.

**Fix:** Use “is”, “are”, or “has” where they express the meaning accurately.

Before: “Gallery 825 serves as LAAA's exhibition space. The gallery features four separate spaces.”

After: “Gallery 825 is LAAA's exhibition space. It has four galleries.”

### 2.3 Vague connection or association

Phrases such as “was associated with leadership of”, “in connection with”, or “connected to” obscure a relationship that can be stated plainly.

**Fix:** State the relationship. For example: “In 2017 she was CEO of ExampleCorp.”

### 2.4 Negative parallelisms

These phrases correct a misconception nobody raised: “It's not just X, it's Y”; “Not only … but also …”; “This isn't A, it's B”; “Not X, but Y”; “X is not a mirror but a portal”; “No X, no Y, just Z”; or overused “Y rather than X”.

**Fix:** State the positive claim alone. Keep the contrast only when readers would reasonably assume the opposite.

Before: “It's not just a coffee shop, it's a gathering place.”

After: “People stay for hours.”

### 2.5 Rule of three

Triplets of adjectives or short phrases are stacked to sound thorough, such as “fast, reliable, and secure” or “learn, connect, and grow”.

**Fix:** Keep a list when its items are actual facts. Otherwise choose the item that matters most, keep two, or vary the rhythm.

### 2.6 Elegant variation

Synonyms are cycled to avoid repeating a noun: “the artist … the painter … the creative … the visionary”.

**Fix:** Repeat the plain noun or use a pronoun.

## 3. Style and formatting

### 3.1 Title Case headings

Headings are capitalised as if every major word were a title, such as “Impact Of Technology And Digitalization”.

**Fix:** Use sentence case unless a house style says otherwise.

### 3.2 Overuse of boldface

Key phrases are bolded throughout in a “key takeaways” style.

**Fix:** Usually leave body prose unbolded, or bold a term at its definition if it helps.

### 3.3 Inline-header vertical lists

Bullets start with a bold label and colon, such as “**Scalability:** The system…”.

**Fix:** Turn the list into prose, use a plain list without labels, or use a real table if the information is tabular.

### 3.4 Overuse of em dashes

Em dashes are used where commas, colons, parentheses, or full stops would be more natural, often with spaces around the dash.

**Fix:** Rewrite with ordinary punctuation. Do not substitute an en dash or hyphen merely to preserve the same pattern. One or two em dashes in a long piece may be fine.

### 3.5 Emoji as formatting

Emoji are placed before headings or bullets.

**Fix:** Remove them when they serve only as decoration.

### 3.6 Unusual small tables

Two- or three-row tables are used where one sentence would read better.

**Fix:** Use prose if a table does not make the information easier to compare.

### 3.7 Curly quotes and apostrophes

Curly and straight quotation marks are mixed in the same text. This alone proves nothing, since word processors also produce curly quotes.

**Fix:** Match the surrounding text's convention.

### 3.8 Thematic breaks between every section

Horizontal rules such as `---` separate every section.

**Fix:** Remove them unless the format needs them.

## 4. Text meant for the chat user

### 4.1 Collaborative communication left in

Examples include “I hope this helps”, “Certainly!”, “Great question”, “Let me know if…”, “Would you like me to…”, “Here is a…”, and “In this section, we will discuss…”.

**Fix:** Delete the conversational residue when it does not belong in the requested deliverable.

### 4.2 Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers and speculation about gaps

Examples include “As of my last update…”, “While specific details are limited…”, “not widely documented/disclosed”, “based on available information”, and “maintains a low profile”, followed by guesses about what missing information “likely” is.

**Fix:** If something genuinely is unknown, say so once, concretely, and tell the reader what to do. For example: “Opening hours aren't posted; call ahead.” Do not pad the gap with speculation.

### 4.3 Placeholders

Examples include “[Your Name]”, “[Describe the section]”, “INSERT_URL”, and “2025-XX-XX”.

**Fix:** Fill them in or remove them.

## 5. Older-model habits

### 5.1 Didactic disclaimers

Examples include “It's important to note/remember/consider”, “It is worth noting”, “may vary”, and “keep in mind”.

**Fix:** State the thing directly. Instead of “Hours may vary”, say “Hours change in winter” if that is what the evidence supports.

### 5.2 Section summaries

Paragraphs end by restating themselves, or openers such as “In summary”, “In conclusion”, “Overall”, and “Ultimately” announce a summary that adds no new information.

**Fix:** End on the last new fact.

## 6. What human writing does instead

The guide points to these tendencies:

- Plain “is/has” sentences: “there is a”, “it has a”.
- Plain words: “wrote” instead of “authored”, “moved” instead of “relocated”, “used” instead of “utilised”, “tried” instead of “attempted”, “died” instead of “passed away”, and “before” instead of “prior to”.
- Definite statements where true: “is the only”, “was the first”, “one of the best”.
- Ordinary hedges and intensifiers: “very”, “perhaps”, “tends to”.
- Specific, unusual facts. Generic phrasing such as “a revolutionary titan of industry” can lose a detail like “inventor of the first train-coupling device”.

## 7. Not signs: don't “fix” these

The guide lists these as ineffective indicators. Changing them wastes effort and can make writing worse:

- Perfect grammar.
- Mixing casual and formal registers.
- “Bland” or formal prose in general, or long words in general.
- A single transition word on its own.
- Unsourced content.
- Correct formatting.
- Quoted material. A real person's words or a title is not rewritten to remove signs.

## The core test

Ask what the sentence contributes. If it gives a fact, preserve it. If it adds unsupported praise, repeats an idea, hides a simple relationship, or interprets a fact without evidence, revise or remove that part. Do not invent details to fill the space.
